Part 2

Should the customer wait for it, or can it happen in the background?

This is one of the most important design decisions in any flow — and it's a product decision, not just a technical one.

⏳ Wait for it (synchronous)

The customer can't continue until it's done. Like waiting at the counter while the cashier swipes your card.

  • Use it when the next step depends on the answer: "Did the payment work?"
  • Keep these steps few and fast — every one adds waiting time.

📬 In the background (asynchronous)

The job is dropped in a queue (Week 2) and done a moment later. Like the waiter pinning your order on the kitchen rail and walking away.

  • Use it when the customer doesn't need to wait: receipts, notifications, analytics.
  • Show progress instead: "Restaurant is preparing your food 🍳".

🔁 When a background job fails: retry, then the "problem box"

Background jobs fail sometimes (the SMS company is slow, a server restarts). Good systems retry a few times, waiting a little longer each time (remember backoff from Week 7). If a job still fails, it goes into a special "problem box" — engineers call it a dead-letter queue — where someone can look at it, instead of it being lost or blocking everything else.

Part 3

Sequence diagrams and "undo steps"

A sequence diagram shows who talks to whom, in what order. It's the drawing TPMs and engineers use most to agree on a flow. You read it from top to bottom, like a comic strip.

Lifeline

Each system gets a column with a line going down.

Solid arrow — a request

"Please do this." Often the sender waits.

Dashed arrow — the answer

"Done" (or "failed"), sent back.

Arrow with 📬 — background

Dropped in a queue; nobody waits.

2. Charge ₦6,500
3. Paid ✅

↩️ Undo steps: when something fails halfway

In Week 2 you learned big apps are made of separate services. There's no single "undo button" across all of them. So if a later step fails, the system must run an undo step for each earlier step that already happened. Engineers call this the saga pattern, and the undo steps compensating actions.

✅ Order saved
→
✅ Card charged ₦6,500
→
❌ Restaurant: "Sorry, sold out"
↩️ Mark order cancelled
←
↩️ Refund ₦6,500 automatically
←

🎓 Without the undo steps, Amaka pays for food that never comes — and has to fight support for a refund. A TPM lists these "what if it fails here?" cases in the PRD.

Part 5

Real examples: Uber Eats and Amazon

Two companies famous for making complicated journeys feel simple to the customer.

The story: every Uber Eats order joins up three different people, each with their own app: the customer, the restaurant and the courier. Each one only sees their part — but the system must keep all three in step.

📱 Customer

  • Pays, then sees live status updates: accepted, cooking, on the way.
  • Most of what happens next is in the background — the customer just watches progress.

🍳 Restaurant

  • A tablet alerts the kitchen and they accept the order.
  • How long the food takes to cook matters for every step after it.

🛵 Courier timing

  • Uber has written about predicting how long food takes to prepare.
  • The goal: the courier arrives when the food is ready — not 15 minutes early (wasting their time) or late (cold food).

🔄 When plans change

  • A courier cancels, an item is out of stock, traffic is bad.
  • Each case needs a planned response: find another courier, offer a swap or refund, update the arrival time.
🎓 TPM lesson from Uber Eats: when several people depend on one flow, map it for each of them. A step that's "done" for the customer might be just starting for the courier.

The story: in 1999 Amazon was granted a patent for 1-Click ordering: buy with a single tap using saved details. Behind that one click is a long chain — payment, stock, warehouse picking, packing and delivery.

⚡ Fast confirmation, slow work later

  • The customer gets "Order placed" quickly.
  • Picking, packing and shipping happen afterwards, in the background, with email updates along the way.

🤖 Robots in the warehouse

  • In 2012 Amazon bought Kiva Systems, a company making warehouse robots (now Amazon Robotics).
  • Robots bring shelves of products to the people who pack them — another system that must join the flow.

📦 Stock must be right

  • Two people shouldn't be able to buy the last item.
  • If it happens anyway, an undo step is needed: cancel, refund, and apologise.

💬 Keep the customer informed

  • Each background step sends an update: shipped, out for delivery, delivered.
  • Progress updates make waiting feel shorter — and cut support calls.
🎓 TPM lesson from Amazon: make the customer's part short and certain, and move everything else into the background — with clear progress updates.

Saturday class

Questions we will discuss together

Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

1. Why not make every step "wait for it", so we're sure everything worked before saying "confirmed"?

A good answer: Because the customer would stare at a spinner for minutes, and one slow partner (like the SMS company) would block every order. Only wait for what the customer truly needs to know now — usually "is it paid?". Everything else happens in the background, with live status updates.

2. The card was charged, then the restaurant rejected the order. Whose job is it to fix this?

A good answer: The system's — automatically. If a person has to notice and refund manually, many customers will wait days (or never get their money). The TPM's job is to make sure this case is in the PRD, with an automatic refund and a clear message, before launch.

3. How can a sequence diagram help in a meeting with engineers?

A good answer: It puts everyone's understanding on one page. People can point at an arrow and ask "what happens if this fails?" or "does the customer wait here?". Disagreements show up in minutes, instead of weeks later in code.

4. What should happen to a background job that has failed 5 times?

A good answer: Stop retrying (so it doesn't overload anything), put it in the dead-letter queue (the "problem box"), and alert someone. Then decide: fix and re-send, or handle it another way (for example, show the receipt in the app if the SMS can't be sent).
