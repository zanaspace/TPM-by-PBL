Part 1

What is a Technical Product Manager?

A Product Manager (PM) decides what to build and why.

A Technical Product Manager (TPM) does that too — but also understands how the product works on the inside, so they can make better decisions with engineers.

🎯 What a TPM does every day

  • Talks with engineers and understands what they are saying.
  • Spots problems early — like "this will be slow when 1 million people use it".
  • Makes trade-offs — "Do we launch fast, or launch later but more solid?"
  • Writes clear plans so engineers know exactly what to build.

You do not need to write code every day. You need to understand how the pieces fit.

💡 Same problem, two ways of thinking

A user complains: "Searching for songs in the app is very slow."

A PM might say:

"Let's show a loading animation so users know we are searching."

A TPM also asks:

"Why is it slow? Is the app searching through millions of songs one by one? Can we keep popular searches ready so they come back instantly?"

The TPM fixes the cause, not just the symptom.

Part 2

Who does what? PM vs TPM vs Engineering Manager

These three people work closely together. Think of building a house:

🧭

Product Manager (PM)

Like the person who decides what kind of house the family needs.

Main question: "What should we build, and why?"

Works with: users, designers, marketing, sales, leaders

Writes: product plans, user stories, user research

🛠️

Technical PM (TPM)

Like the person who checks the pipes, wiring and foundation will hold up.

Main question: "Can the system handle this, and what does it depend on?"

Works with: engineers, data teams, outside partners

Writes: technical plans, how data moves, what can go wrong

👷

Engineering Manager (EM)

Like the head builder who leads the builders and checks the work.

Main question: "How do we build it well and on time?"

Works with: developers, testers

Writes: build plans, task lists, code reviews

Part 3

The 4 parts of every app

Almost every app is made of the same 4 parts. The easiest way to remember them is to think of a restaurant.

1📱

Frontend

What you see and touch: screens, buttons, the map.

🍽️ Restaurant: the dining room and the waiter who takes your order.
Examples: the Uber app on your phone, Spotify on your laptop.
2⚙️

Backend

The "brain" you can't see. It follows the rules and makes decisions.

👨‍🍳 Restaurant: the kitchen, where the cooks prepare your order.
Examples: finding the nearest driver, checking your password.
3🗄️

Database

The app's memory. It saves information so it is there tomorrow.

📦 Restaurant: the storeroom and the order book.
Examples: your account, past trips, your playlists.
4🤝

Outside services

Other companies' tools the app "borrows" instead of building them.

🚚 Restaurant: suppliers — the fish seller, the gas supplier, the POS machine.
Examples: Google Maps, Paystack (payments), SMS codes.
🔌

How do the parts talk? Through an API.

An API is like the order slip the waiter carries to the kitchen. It has a fixed format, so the kitchen always understands it. When the Uber app asks the backend "find me a driver", it sends that request through an API.

You will learn much more about APIs in Week 3. For now: API = the agreed way two parts of software talk to each other.

🎓 Why a TPM cares about outside services: your app depends on them, but you don't control them. If Paystack is down, can people still book a ride? If SMS codes are late, can people still log in? A good TPM plans a "Plan B" for each one.

Part 5

Real examples: Uber and Spotify, broken into parts

This is exactly what you will do in your assignment: take a real app and sort it into the 4 parts.

What it does: connects people who need a ride with drivers nearby — in real time, for millions of people at once.

📱 Frontend

  • Two apps: one for riders, one for drivers.
  • The driver's app sends its location every few seconds.
  • Must not crash if the phone loses network in a tunnel.

⚙️ Backend

  • Matching: finds the nearest free driver.
  • Pricing: raises prices when many people want rides and few drivers are free ("surge").
  • Splits the city into small areas so it only searches near you, not the whole world.

🗄️ Database

  • Saves rider and driver accounts, trips and receipts.
  • Keeps driver locations in very fast, short-term memory (they change every few seconds).

🤝 Outside services

  • Maps: routes, traffic and arrival time.
  • Payments: charges your card at the end of the trip.
  • Calls / SMS: lets rider and driver call without seeing each other's real number.
🎓 TPM lesson from Uber: speed matters. If finding a driver takes too long, people leave. So Uber only searches the small area around you, and keeps driver locations in fast memory.

What it does: lets you play almost any song instantly, on any device, even on a weak network.

📱 Frontend

  • Apps for phone, computer, web, TV and car.
  • Saves recently played songs on your phone so they start instantly.
  • Starts loading the next song before the current one ends.

⚙️ Backend

  • Checks if you are Free or Premium (what you are allowed to play).
  • Makes recommendations like "Discover Weekly" from what similar people enjoy.
  • Counts plays longer than 30 seconds so artists get paid.

🗄️ Database

  • Stores song details: title, artist, album, playlists.
  • The music files themselves are kept in a separate large file store — not in the database.

🤝 Outside services

  • Delivery networks (CDN): copies of songs kept on computers in many cities, so the music comes from somewhere close to you.
  • Payments: for Premium subscriptions.
🎓 TPM lesson from Spotify: keep the small information (song name, artist) separate from the big files (the actual music). Small details go in the database; big files go to storage close to the user.

Saturday class

Questions we will discuss together

Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

1. Uber sends you a code by SMS to log in. What if the SMS company stops working?

A good answer: Never let one outside company lock all your users out. Have a Plan B: send the code by WhatsApp or email instead, or switch to a second SMS company. Also show the user a clear message like "Code delayed — try WhatsApp instead", not just an error.

2. Why doesn't Spotify keep the music files inside its main database?

A good answer: A database is great for small pieces of information (names, prices, playlists). Music files are big. If millions of people pulled big files from the database at once, it would become very slow. So the files live in a separate store, with copies placed close to users in many cities.

3. How can a TPM tell if the backend is "healthy"?

A good answer: Watch four simple things:

  • Speed: how long does it take to answer? (called latency)
  • Traffic: how many requests is it getting right now?
  • Errors: how many requests are failing?
  • How full it is: are the computers close to their limit? (called saturation)
