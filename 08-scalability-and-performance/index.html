An app that works for 2,000 people can fall over when 60,000 arrive at once — usually at the worst moment, like a big sale. Part 1

Handling more people: scale up or scale out?

Scalability = can the app keep working as more and more people use it?

Performance = how fast it feels for each person. Users notice after about 1 second, and many leave after 3.

⬆️ Scale up (vertical): a bigger computer

🍳 Restaurant: buy a bigger stove for your one cook.

  • Good: simple — no changes to how the app is built.
  • Watch out: there's a limit to how big one computer can get, big ones get very expensive, and upgrading often means a short shutdown.
  • Risk: if that one computer fails, everything stops.

➡️ Scale out (horizontal): more computers

👩🏾‍🍳 Restaurant: hire more cooks, each with their own stove.

  • Good: add as many as you need; if one fails, the others keep going.
  • Good: with auto-scaling, servers are added when it gets busy and removed when it's quiet — you only pay for what you use.
  • Watch out: the app must be built so any server can handle any customer.

Part 2

Caching and CDNs: don't do the same work twice

🍲

Cache = food on the warmer

If 500 people order jollof every lunchtime, keep a pot ready instead of cooking each plate from scratch. A cache keeps popular answers ready in fast memory, so the database isn't asked the same question again and again.

⏲️

TTL = when to throw it out

Time To Live is how long a cached answer is kept. Long TTL = less work, but data may be old (stale). Short TTL = always fresh, but more work. Choosing it is a product decision!

🌍

CDN = branches near customers

A Content Delivery Network keeps copies of images and files on servers in many cities. A customer in Lagos gets photos from Lagos, not from a server in Europe — faster, and your own servers do less work.

Part 3

When the database gets too busy

The database (the app's memory, from Week 1) is usually the first part to break, because almost every request needs it. Most apps read far more than they write — people browse the menu 20 times for every order they place. That shapes the fix.

📚 Read replicas: copies for reading

Keep one main database for writing (new orders, payments) and several copies that are only used for reading (menus, order history). The main one keeps the copies up to date.

🍽️ Like giving each waiter a photocopy of the menu book, instead of all of them queueing for the one original.

⚠️ Copies can be a second or two behind. Fine for menus — not for a bank balance.

✂️ Sharding: split the data

When even writing is too much for one database, split the data across several databases. For example, customers whose names start with A–M go to database 1, and N–Z go to database 2.

⚠️ Powerful but complex. Big apps like Instagram (Week 2) do it; most apps should try caching and replicas first.

🍾 The bottleneck: the narrowest part decides

Water can only pour out of a bottle as fast as its neck allows. A system is only as fast as its slowest part. Adding 10 more app servers does nothing if the database is the one choking. A TPM's first question during a slowdown is: "Which part is at 100%?"

        Part 5

        Real examples: Shopify and Twitter

        One company plans for its busiest weekend of the year. The other solved a very unusual problem: a few accounts with enormous audiences.

        The story: millions of online shops run on Shopify. On Black Friday and Cyber Monday, all of them have their biggest sales at the same time, and Shopify handles tens of millions of requests per minute at the peak. Being down for even a few minutes would cost shop owners a fortune.

        🏘️ "Pods": separate neighbourhoods

        • Shopify splits shops into groups called pods, each with its own databases.
        • If one huge shop has a massive flash sale, it mostly affects its own pod — not every shop on Shopify.

        🧪 Practise the big day

        • For months before, teams run load tests: pretend traffic bigger than they expect.
        • They find the bottleneck before customers do — just like the load-test button in the simulation.

        🧊 A "freeze" before the rush

        • Many companies stop risky changes in the days before a huge event.
        • Fewer changes = fewer surprises (remember Week 7's Friday night).

        🚦 Waiting rooms

        • Many shops and ticket sites use a "waiting room" when demand is extreme: you queue for a moment instead of everyone getting an error.
        • A short, honest wait beats a crashed site.
        🎓 TPM lesson from Shopify: you know when your big days are coming. Plan capacity weeks ahead, load-test, and keep changes small near the event.

        The story: on Twitter (now X), when you post, your followers should see it in their home timeline. For most people that's a few hundred followers. But some accounts have tens of millions of followers. Copying one post into 100 million timelines, instantly, is a huge job.

        📮 Most people: deliver straight away

        • When a normal user posts, the system copies the post into each follower's ready-made timeline right away.
        • So timelines open instantly — reading is fast, because it's prepared in advance.

        ⭐ Celebrities: collect when you open the app

        • Posts from accounts with enormous followings are not copied to everyone.
        • Instead, they're mixed into your timeline when you open it.

        ⚖️ The trade-off

        • Prepare in advance = fast reading, heavy writing.
        • Collect on demand = light writing, slower reading. Twitter uses each where it fits best.

        🔗 Same idea as Instagram

        • You saw the same pattern in Week 2 with Instagram's celebrities.
        • Big social apps keep arriving at the same answer.
        🎓 TPM lesson from Twitter: averages hide the hard cases. Ask "what about our biggest user?" — the busiest restaurant, the most-followed account, the largest order.

        Saturday class

        Questions we will discuss together

        Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

        1. The app is slow, and the CEO says "just buy more servers". What do you ask first?

        A good answer: "Which part is actually at its limit?" If the database is the bottleneck, more app servers won't help — they'll just send more requests to the struggling database. Find the narrowest part first, then fix that.

        2. Why not just cache everything for a whole day?

        A good answer: Because customers would see old information — sold-out food still for sale, old prices, closed restaurants that look open. That leads to cancellations and angry customers. Things that change often, or that involve money, need short TTLs or no cache at all.

        3. Auto-scaling sounds perfect. Is there any downside?

        A good answer: New servers take time to start, so a very sudden spike can arrive faster than they do — you may still need some extra servers ready before a planned event. Costs can also surprise you if a bug or attack creates fake traffic, so teams set a maximum.

        4. Nothing you do will handle 10 million users at once. What should the product do?

        A good answer: Fail gracefully. Show a waiting room ("You're in line — about 2 minutes") instead of errors, keep the most important actions working (like paying for orders already started), and turn off nice-to-have features for a while. A TPM decides in advance what matters most.
