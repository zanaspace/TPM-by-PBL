Part 1

Systems thinking: everything is connected

Systems thinking means looking at the whole product, not just one screen or one feature.

The parts of an app are joined together. When you change or break one part, other parts feel it — like dominoes falling.

💳Payment check gets slow
→
⏳Orders pile up waiting
→
🔥Servers get too busy
→
🐢Even the menu loads slowly
→
😡Customers leave

One slow part caused a problem in a part that "had nothing to do with it". A TPM spots these chains before they happen.

🔗

1. Chain reactions

A problem in one place travels to other places. Always ask: "If this part is slow or broken, what else is affected?"

🧩

2. Keep parts loosely joined

Parts should depend on each other as little as possible. If the SMS service fails, people should still be able to order food. Engineers call this loose coupling.

⚖️

3. You can't have everything

When parts of a system can't talk to each other for a moment, you must choose: show slightly old information, or show an error until you are sure. This choice is called the CAP trade-off.

Part 2

3 ways to build an app

Architecture just means how the parts of an app are arranged. Engineers argue about this a lot. Your job as a TPM is not to pick the "coolest" one — it is to understand the trade-offs for the business. Let's use food businesses again.

🏠

Monolith

The whole app is one single piece. Login, menu, payments and messages all live in one program.

🍳 Like: one big kitchen where every cook shares the same room and the same gas cylinder.
👍 Good
  • Simple and quick to build
  • Easy to find problems
  • Cheap for a small team
👎 Watch out
  • A bug in one corner can crash everything
  • To handle more of one thing, you copy the whole app
  • Big teams get in each other's way

Real life: most startups begin here. Shopify and Stack Overflow still run very large monoliths.

🏬

Microservices

The app is split into many small apps ("services"), each doing one job. They talk to each other through APIs.

🍢 Like: a food court. The rice stall, grill stall and drinks stall each have their own staff and kitchen.
👍 Good
  • If one service breaks, the others keep working
  • Add power only to the busy part
  • Teams can work and release on their own
👎 Watch out
  • Many more things to watch and manage
  • Passing work between services adds small delays
  • Harder to find where a problem started

Real life: Netflix, Uber and Amazon moved to this when they grew very big.

📬

Event-driven (with a queue)

Instead of waiting for an answer, one part drops a message in a queue and moves on. Another part picks it up when it is ready.

🧾 Like: the order rail in a busy kitchen. The waiter pins the ticket and walks away. A cook takes it when free.
👍 Good
  • Nobody stands around waiting
  • Handles sudden rushes well — work just waits its turn
  • If a part is down, messages wait safely
👎 Watch out
  • Things happen "a bit later", not instantly
  • The same message could be handled twice by mistake
  • Harder to follow the full journey

Real life: order-confirmation emails, SMS alerts and "your report is ready" messages usually work this way.

🎓 Important: these are not "either / or". Most big apps mix them — for example, microservices that use queues for messages. A good rule: start simple (monolith), and split parts out only when there is a real reason — like one part being much busier, or many teams getting in each other's way.

    How apps store data: tables, keys and links (ERD)

    The database is the app's memory. Most databases store information in tables — just like spreadsheets in Excel or Google Sheets. An ERD (Entity Relationship Diagram) is a simple drawing of those tables and how they are linked.

    📋

    Entity = a table

    A "thing" the app must remember. For example User, Post, Order. Each becomes one table.

    🏷️

    Attribute = a column

    A detail about the thing. A User has a username, email and date_joined.

    🔑

    Primary key (PK)

    A unique ID for each row, so no two are mixed up. Like your matric number or NIN.

    🔗

    Foreign key (FK)

    A column that points to another table's ID. Like writing a student's matric number on a library card to show who borrowed the book.

    The 3 kinds of links (relationships)

    1 : 1

    One-to-one

    Each person has one NIN, and each NIN belongs to one person.

    In an app: one User ↔ one Profile settings page.

    1 : many

    One-to-many

    One mother can have many children, but each child has one (birth) mother.

    In an app: one User → many Posts. One Restaurant → many Menu items.

    many : many

    Many-to-many

    A student takes many courses, and a course has many students. You need a middle table (like a class register) to connect them.

    In an app: Users ↔ Users who follow each other (the middle table is follows).

    Real examples: Netflix and Instagram

    Two famous apps, two different problems. Look for the systems thinking in each story.

    The story: in 2008, one broken database stopped Netflix from sending DVDs for 3 days. One part failed — and the whole business stopped. After that, Netflix spent about 7 years moving from one big app to hundreds of small services running in the cloud.

    🏬 Many small services

    • Separate services for login, search, recommendations, playing video, billing and more.
    • A "front door" service (called a gateway) receives every request and sends it to the right service.

    🛟 A "Plan B" for every part

    • If the "recommended for you" service fails, Netflix does not show an error.
    • It quietly shows a saved list of popular movies instead. Most users never notice!

    🐒 Breaking things on purpose

    • Netflix built a tool called Chaos Monkey that randomly switches off its own services.
    • Why? To prove the app keeps working when parts fail — before a real failure happens.

    📦 Movies stored close to you

    • Netflix puts its own boxes full of movies inside internet companies in many cities.
    • So most video travels a short distance, not across the world.
    🎓 TPM lesson from Netflix: design so that one broken part can't stop the whole product. For each part, ask: "What do we show users if this fails?"

    The story: on Instagram, a photo is posted once but viewed hundreds or millions of times. So the system is built to make reading (scrolling the feed) extremely fast, even if writing (posting) does a bit more work.

    🗄️ The main tables

    • users — who you are.
    • posts — what was posted, and by which user.
    • media — where the photo file is stored (not the photo itself).
    • likes and follows — the links between people and posts.

    📮 Your friend posts → feeds get ready early

    • When a normal user posts, Instagram drops the post into each follower's feed straight away — like putting a letter in 300 mailboxes.
    • So when you open the app, your feed is already prepared.

    ⭐ Celebrities are handled differently

    • Cristiano Ronaldo has over 600 million followers. Putting one post into 600 million mailboxes would take too long.
    • Instead, celebrity posts are added to your feed when you open the app.

    ✂️ Splitting the database

    • One database can't hold billions of posts, so Instagram splits the data across thousands of databases (called sharding).
    • Each post ID even contains a clue about which database it lives in.
    🎓 TPM lesson from Instagram: ask early, "Will people mostly read or mostly write?" The answer changes how you design the system. One special group (celebrities) may need its own plan.

    Questions we will discuss together

    Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

    1. Your startup has 3 engineers and no customers yet. An engineer wants to build 12 microservices. What do you say?

    A good answer: Start with a well-organised monolith. With 3 people, 12 services means lots of extra setup and things to watch, and it slows you down when speed matters most. Keep the code tidy inside, so parts can be split out later if one becomes very busy.

    2. In the simulation, why did breaking "Messages" crash everything in the monolith, but not with microservices + queue?

    A good answer: In a monolith, everything runs as one program — when one part crashes, the whole program goes down. With microservices, messages run separately. With a queue, the order service doesn't even wait for the message to be sent. It drops it in the queue and moves on. When Messages comes back, the queue sends them all.

    3. A user changes their username. Why doesn't the app need to update every one of their old posts?

    A good answer: Posts don't store the username — they store the user's ID (a foreign key). The username lives in only one place: the users table. Change it there once, and every post shows the new name. Storing each fact in one place avoids mistakes.

    4. Which is worse for a bank app: showing an old balance, or showing "Please try again"?

    A good answer: Showing an old balance can be dangerous — the person might spend money they don't have. For money, it is better to say "try again" than to be wrong. But for a like count on Instagram, an old number is fine. The right choice depends on the cost of being wrong.
