Part 1

What is Agile?

Agile means building a product in small pieces, showing each piece to real people, and improving as you go.

The most common way to do it is Scrum: the team works in fixed rounds of about 2 weeks, called sprints.

🍲 Cooking for a wedding: two ways

The old way ("Waterfall")

Cook every dish for 6 hours, then taste for the first time when guests arrive. If the jollof is too salty, it's too late.

The Agile way

Cook one pot, taste it, adjust, then cook the next. Problems are found early, when they are cheap to fix.

📚 5 words you'll hear every day

  • Backlog — the full to-do list of everything the product might need, in priority order.
  • Ticket / user story — one piece of work on that list, e.g. "Save card for next time".
  • Sprint — a 2-week round. The team picks some tickets and finishes them.
  • Sprint goal — the one main thing this sprint must achieve.
  • Board — a screen with columns (To do → In progress → In review → Done) showing where each ticket is.

Part 2

The 4 sprint meetings — and what the TPM does

Scrum calls these meetings ceremonies. Each one has a clear job. Long, pointless meetings are a sign something is wrong.

📋Day 1 • about 1 hour

1. Sprint planning

"What will we finish in these 2 weeks?"

TPM's job: bring the sprint goal and the top tickets, ready to build. Help the team pick only what fits — and say clearly what is left out.

☀️Every day • 15 minutes

2. Daily standup

"What did I do yesterday, what will I do today, is anything blocking me?"

TPM's job: listen for blockers — and remove them that same day (chase another team, a partner, a decision).

🎬Last day • about 1 hour

3. Sprint review (demo)

"Here is what we built — what do you think?"

TPM's job: show working features to stakeholders, collect feedback, and update the backlog.

🔁Last day • 45 minutes

4. Retrospective (retro)

"How did we work together? What should we change?"

TPM's job: keep it honest and blame-free, and turn it into 1–2 real action items for next sprint.

➕ Many teams also hold backlog refinement once a week: going through upcoming tickets so they are clear and sized before planning day.

Part 3

Sizing and choosing work

📏 Story points: size, not hours

Instead of guessing hours, teams compare tickets: "Is this bigger or smaller than the login screen we built?" — like saying a watermelon is "about 5 oranges" without weighing it.

1Tiny — change a word
2Small — copy an existing pattern
3Medium
5Big — touches app, backend and database
8Very big, some unknowns
13Too big — split it into smaller tickets

The numbers jump (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13) because big things are harder to guess exactly.

🏎️ Velocity: how much fits

Velocity is how many points a team usually finishes in one sprint. If the last 3 sprints were 28, 31 and 30 points, plan about 30 — not 45 because "we'll try hard".

✅ Ready and Done

Definition of Ready — a ticket can start only when it has a clear PRD, designs and acceptance criteria (Week 4!).

Definition of Done — a ticket is done only when the code is reviewed, tested, and working for real users. "Done on my laptop" isn't done.

🧹 The 70 / 20 / 10 rule: don't skip cleaning the kitchen

If a restaurant only cooks and never cleans, it gets slower every week — then the health inspector closes it. Code is the same. Shortcuts pile up as tech debt, and every new feature takes longer. Many teams split each sprint like this:

70% New features
20% Tech debt
10%
Features — things customers will see and use.
Tech debt — cleaning messy code, updating old software, fixing slow parts.
Spikes — short, time-limited research: "Could we take orders on WhatsApp? Find out in 2 days."

It's a guide, not a law. A TPM explains to leaders why that 20% protects future speed.

🍚 How to choose what goes first: RICE

When everything feels important, score each idea. Multiply the good things, divide by the cost:

Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort
Reach How many people does it affect each month?
Impact How much does it help each person? (3 = a lot, 1 = some, 0.5 = a little)
Confidence How sure are we? (100%, 80%, 50%)
Effort How much work? (e.g. person-weeks)

Example: "Save card" reaches 20,000 people × impact 2 × 80% sure ÷ 2 weeks of work = 16,000. "Dark mode" reaches 5,000 × 0.5 × 100% ÷ 3 = 833. Save card goes first.

            Part 5

            Real examples: Linear and Slack

            One company builds a tool for running sprints. The other shows how big teams release work safely, many times a day.

            The story: Linear is a tool for tracking tickets — like Jira, which you'll also meet. Many startups switched to it because it is fast and simple, and it is built around how good teams like to work.

            🔄 Cycles instead of stressful deadlines

            • Linear calls sprints "cycles". They repeat automatically.
            • Unfinished tickets roll into the next cycle by themselves — no blame, no busywork.

            🤖 The board updates itself

            • When an engineer starts work or opens their code for review on GitHub, the ticket moves column on its own.
            • The board stays true without anyone dragging cards around.

            📥 A "triage" inbox

            • New bug reports and requests don't jump straight into the sprint.
            • They land in a triage inbox first. Someone (often the PM/TPM) decides: accept, merge with another, or decline.

            ⚡ Speed is a feature

            • Linear keeps a copy of your data in the browser, so clicks feel instant.
            • Teams use tools more when they're fast — and the board stays up to date.
            🎓 TPM lesson from Linear: protect the sprint. New requests go to a queue to be sorted, not straight onto engineers' desks — just like the CEO request in the simulation.

            The story: millions of workplaces depend on Slack all day. Its engineers release new code many times a day — without breaking work for everyone. How?

            🚩 Feature flags: an on/off switch

            • New code goes live but stays switched off, hidden behind a "flag".
            • The team turns it on for 1% of users, checks the numbers, then 10%, 50%, 100%.

            🐶 Staff try it first

            • New versions go to Slack's own employees before customers.
            • This is called "dogfooding" — eating your own dog food.

            ↩️ Quick switch-off

            • If errors jump after a release, the flag can be turned off in minutes.
            • No emergency rebuild needed — users go back to the old version.

            🤝 Blameless reviews

            • When something breaks, the question is "what check was missing?", not "who did it?".
            • People report problems faster when they aren't afraid of blame.
            🎓 TPM lesson from Slack: "deployed" (the code is live) and "released" (users can see it) are different moments. Flags let a TPM plan launches step by step, and stop quickly if something goes wrong.

            Saturday class

            Questions we will discuss together

            Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

            1. In the middle of a sprint, the CEO asks for a new feature "by Friday". What do you do?

            A good answer: Don't just say yes or no. Ask how urgent it really is, then show the trade-off: "We can add it, but then this comes out and the sprint goal may slip." If it can wait 1–2 weeks, put it at the top of the next sprint. Make the cost visible and let the right person decide.

            2. A team finished only 18 of 30 points. Should they be told to "work harder"?

            A good answer: No — first find out why in the retro. Were they blocked? Were tickets unclear? Did urgent bugs interrupt them? Did they take on too much? Usually the fix is in the process (clearer tickets, fewer surprises, smaller commitments), not in longer hours.

            3. How would you explain to a non-technical leader why 20% of the sprint goes to tech debt?

            A good answer: Compare it to servicing a car, or cleaning a kitchen. Skip it for a while and nothing seems wrong — until the car breaks down on the highway. Show proof where you can: "Since we cleaned up checkout, payment features take half as long."

            4. Is a story point the same as a day of work?

            A good answer: No. Points compare size and risk between tickets. A senior engineer and a new engineer may need different time for the same 5-point ticket. Over a few sprints, velocity tells you how many points this team finishes — that's what you plan with.
