Debugging and Issue Tracking

Every product breaks sometimes. What matters is how fast you notice, how calmly you respond, and what you learn. This week you'll learn to write bug reports engineers can act on, decide what to fix first, and find the real cause of a problem. Then you'll lead a live incident: payments are failing on a Friday night, and the clock is ticking.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • ✓ Write a clear bug report with steps to reproduce, expected and actual results
  • ✓ Give a bug the right priority, from P0 (drop everything) to P3 (later)
  • ✓ Follow the steps of an incident: notice, triage, stop the damage, communicate, fix, learn
  • ✓ Use the "5 Whys" to find a root cause — without blaming people
  • ✓ Write a full bug report for a real app

Part 1

Writing a bug report engineers can act on

A bug is when the product doesn't do what it should. A bug report (or ticket) describes it so an engineer can see it happen for themselves — and fix it.

Think of a doctor: "I feel sick" isn't enough. "Since Tuesday, a fever every evening, worse after eating" helps them find the cause.

😩 A bug report engineers dread

Checkout broken!!!

The app crashed when I tried to pay. Please fix ASAP.

Which app version? Which phone? Which payment method? What exactly happened? The engineer must come back with 5 questions before they can even start.

😊 A bug report engineers love

Paying with a new card shows "Something went wrong" (saved cards work)

Where: Android app 5.4.0, Samsung A14, live app

Steps: 1. Add jollof to basket 2. Checkout 3. Choose "New card" 4. Enter test card 4081… 5. Tap Pay

Expected: Payment succeeds, order confirmed.

Actual: "Something went wrong" after 3 seconds. No charge.

How often: 5 out of 5 tries. Started today around 7:30 PM.

Proof: screen recording + error card_token is required

Part 2

What to fix first: P0 to P3

Teams get more bug reports than they can fix. Priority decides the order. Most teams use 4 levels — like a hospital emergency room deciding who is seen first.

P0

Drop everything

Something core is broken for many people: nobody can pay, log in, or order.

Example: 40% of payments failing.

⏱️ Start now, day or night. Stop the damage within the hour.

P1

Urgent

A big feature is broken for a large group, with no easy way around it.

Example: restaurants get order alerts 5 minutes late.

⏱️ Fix today.

P2

Normal

Something is broken, but there's a workaround, or few people are affected.

Example: a discount code fails the first time but works on retry.

⏱️ Fix this sprint or next.

P3

Later

Small or cosmetic: it looks wrong, but doesn't stop anyone.

Example: a typo on the Help page.

⏱️ Add to the backlog.

🧮 Priority helper

Answer two questions to get a suggested priority.

🤔 Severity vs priority — not always the same

Severity = how bad the bug is. Priority = how soon we fix it. Usually they match, but not always:

  • Low severity, high priority: the company name is misspelt on the home screen on launch day. Nothing breaks — but everyone sees it. Fix now.
  • High severity, lower priority: the app crashes on a phone model only 3 customers use. Bad for them, but other things may come first — and you can contact them directly.

The TPM helps decide priority, because it needs product and business judgement — not just technical judgement.

Part 3

Incidents and root causes

An incident is a serious, live problem — usually a P0 or P1. Good teams follow the same steps every time, so nobody panics.

🔔1. Notice

An alert fires, or customers complain. The faster you notice, the less damage.

🏷️2. Triage

How bad? How many people? Give it a priority and gather the right people.

🧯3. Stop the damage

First stop the bleeding (roll back, switch a feature off). The perfect fix can come later.

📣4. Communicate

Tell customers, support and leaders what's happening and when the next update is.

🛠️5. Fix properly

Now engineers build and test the real fix, without the pressure of a live outage.

📚6. Learn

Write a blameless post-mortem: what happened, why, and what we'll change.

🔎 The 5 Whys: dig past the first answer

Keep asking "why?" until you reach something you can change in how the team works. Example: customers were charged twice.

  1. Why were customers charged twice? The app sent the same payment request twice.
  2. Why did it send it twice? The Pay button stayed active, and people tapped again when it felt slow.
  3. Why didn't the backend notice the repeat? There was no idempotency key (Week 3!).
  4. Why was there no idempotency key? The PRD didn't mention retries or double taps (Week 4!).
  5. Why didn't the PRD mention it? — ROOT CAUSE: Our PRD checklist has no "what if it's sent twice?" question for payment features.
🎓 Blameless: notice the root cause is never "Tobi made a mistake". People will always make mistakes — the question is what system let the mistake reach customers. If people fear blame, they hide problems.

      Part 5

      Real incidents: CrowdStrike and Amazon Web Services

      Two real outages that affected millions of people — and the lessons the companies shared afterwards.

      What happened: on 19 July 2024, CrowdStrike, a security company, sent a faulty update to its software on Windows computers. Microsoft estimated about 8.5 million computers crashed and couldn't restart normally. Airlines, banks, hospitals and TV stations around the world were disrupted.

      💥 One update, everywhere at once

      • The update went to a huge number of computers at nearly the same time.
      • So when it was broken, it broke almost everywhere before anyone could stop it.

      🧯 Hard to undo

      • The faulty update was pulled within about an hour and a half.
      • But many crashed computers needed someone to fix them by hand, one by one — which took days for some companies.

      🔁 What they changed

      • More testing of this kind of update before release.
      • Releasing updates gradually — a small group first, then more — and giving customers more control over when they get them.

      🌍 Why it mattered so much

      • Many companies depended on the same software (a shared dependency — Week 1!).
      • One supplier's mistake became everyone's incident.
      🎓 TPM lesson from CrowdStrike: release risky changes to a small group first, and always know how you would undo them. That's exactly what saves you in this week's simulation.

      What happened: in September 2015, a short network problem in Amazon Web Services' US-East region caused trouble for DynamoDB, a database service thousands of companies use. Many well-known websites and apps had errors for several hours.

      🐘 Everyone asked at once

      • After the network blip, many storage servers asked a central service for information at the same moment.
      • That central service was overwhelmed and answered too slowly.

      🔁 Retrying made it worse

      • When answers were too slow, servers gave up and tried again — adding even more load.
      • Engineers call this a retry storm or "thundering herd".

      🧯 How they recovered

      • AWS reduced the load on the central service and added more capacity.
      • Then services slowly came back to normal.

      ⏳ The general fix: wait smarter

      • When something fails, wait a little longer before each retry — and add a small random delay so everyone doesn't retry together.
      • This is called "backoff with jitter".
      🎓 TPM lesson from AWS: recovery can be dangerous too. When planning for failure, ask "what happens when everything comes back at the same time?"

      Saturday class

      Questions we will discuss together

      Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

      1. During an incident, an engineer wants to spend an hour finding the perfect fix. What do you say?

      A good answer: "First, can we stop the damage right now?" — roll back the last change, or switch the feature flag off. Customers are being hurt every minute. Once things are stable, the perfect fix can be built and tested calmly.

      2. What makes a good status update for customers during an outage?

      A good answer: Plain words, honesty, what still works, and when the next update is. For example: "Some card payments are failing. Saved cards and pay-on-delivery still work. We're fixing it — next update by 8:30 PM." No technical jargon, no blaming a partner, no silence.

      3. Why should post-mortems be "blameless"?

      A good answer: Because if people are punished for mistakes, they hide them, and you learn nothing. Everyone makes mistakes. A good post-mortem asks which check, test or process was missing — and fixes that, so the next person can't make the same mistake.

      4. A customer reports a bug you can't make happen again. Is it real?

      A good answer: Probably — some bugs only happen on certain phones, slow networks, or with certain accounts. Ask for details (device, app version, time, screenshot), check if other customers report the same, and look at the error logs for that time. "Can't reproduce" means "keep investigating", not "close it".
