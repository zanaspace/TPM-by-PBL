Writing a Technical PRD

A PRD (Product Requirements Document) is the plan that tells the whole team what to build, why, and how to know it's done. A vague PRD leads to the wrong thing being built, delays, and angry customers. This week you will learn to write a clear one — then write a PRD yourself and watch what happens on launch week.

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • ✓ Explain what a PRD is, who reads it, and what goes in it
  • ✓ Tell the difference between "what it does" (functional) and "how well it does it" (non-functional) requirements
  • ✓ Turn a vague wish ("it should be fast") into something measurable
  • ✓ Write acceptance criteria using Given – When – Then
  • ✓ Write a full PRD for one feature of your product

Part 1

What is a PRD?

A PRD is like the architect's plan for a house. Before anyone lays a brick, everyone agrees what is being built, for whom, and what "finished" looks like.

A Technical PRD adds the parts engineers need: how fast it must be, what can go wrong, and how the systems talk (the APIs from Week 3).

👥 Who reads your PRD?

  • Engineers — to know exactly what to build.
  • Designers — to design the right screens, including error screens.
  • QA testers — to know how to check that it works.
  • Data analysts — to measure if it succeeded.
  • Leaders — to decide if it is worth the time and money.

If any of them has to guess, the PRD isn't finished.

💡 Same feature, two PRDs

A vague PRD says:

"Let guests book homes instantly."

A technical PRD also says:

"If two guests tap Book for the same dates at the same moment, only one booking succeeds. The other sees 'These dates were just booked' within 1 second. No one is charged twice."

The 7 parts of a good PRD

1
ProblemWho has the problem, and how do we know? (data, interviews)
2
Success metricA number that shows it worked: "abandonment falls from 25% to 15%".
3
ScopeWhat's in this version — and, just as important, what's not.
4
Functional requirementsWhat the product must do.
5
Non-functional requirementsHow well it must work: speed, scale, safety.
6
Acceptance criteriaExact tests that prove it's done, written as Given – When – Then.
7
What can go wrongEdge cases and failures, and what happens in each.
Part 2

"What it does" vs "how well it does it"

Requirements come in two types. Think of ordering a car from a factory:

⚙️ Functional requirements (FR) — what it does

🚗 Car: "It has 4 doors, air conditioning and a radio."

  • FR-1: Customers can choose a delivery time slot for today or tomorrow.
  • FR-2: Each restaurant can set the maximum orders per 30-minute slot.
  • FR-3: Customers get an SMS 15 minutes before their slot.

📏 Non-functional requirements (NFR) — how well

🚗 Car: "It reaches 100 km/h in 10 seconds and passes the crash test."

  • Speed: the slot list loads in under 1 second for 95% of users.
  • Scale: it keeps working with 3,000 orders between 12 and 1 PM.
  • Reliability: available 99.9% of the time (under 45 minutes down per month).
  • Security: card details are never stored on ChopNow's servers.

🔧 The golden rule: make it measurable

If you can't measure it, engineers can't build it and testers can't check it. Swap feelings for numbers:

❌ "It should be fast."→✅ "Loads in under 2 seconds on a 3G connection for 95% of users."
❌ "It must handle lots of users."→✅ "Works with 3,000 orders in the busiest hour."
❌ "It should be secure."→✅ "Card numbers are never saved; only the payment company's token is stored."
❌ "Users should love it."→✅ "App-store rating for this flow stays at 4.5 or above."

Part 3

Acceptance criteria: Given – When – Then

Acceptance criteria are the exact checks that prove a feature is finished. The team agrees on them before building, so nobody argues later. The most common way to write them has 3 parts (engineers call this format Gherkin):

Given

the starting situation

"Given the 12:30 slot already has 10 orders…"
When

the thing someone does

"When a customer opens the list of slots…"
Then

what must happen — something you can check

"Then the 12:30 slot shows 'Full' and can't be picked."
📄 Example: two people want the last slot
Scenario: Two customers try to book the last slot at the same moment
  Given Mama Put Express allows 10 orders in the 12:30 slot
  And   the 12:30 slot already has 9 orders
  When  Amaka and Tunde both tap "12:30" within the same second
  Then  only one of them gets the slot
  And   the other sees "This slot was just taken — 12:45 is free" within 1 second
  And   the 12:30 slot never has more than 10 orders

💡 Always write at least one happy path (everything goes right) and one edge case (something unusual or broken). Bugs live in the edge cases.

Part 5

Real examples: Airbnb and WhatsApp

Two famous features where the hard part wasn't the button — it was the requirements behind it.

The story: on Airbnb, guests used to send a request and wait — sometimes a whole day — for the host to say yes. Many gave up. Instant Book lets guests book straight away. Simple idea, but the PRD had to answer some hard questions.

🎯 Problem and metric

  • Problem: guests leave while waiting for a host's reply.
  • Metric: more searches turn into confirmed bookings.

⚔️ Edge case: two guests, same dates

  • If two guests tap Book at the same moment, the system must let only one win.
  • The other must get a clear message straight away — and must not be charged.

🛡️ Protecting hosts

  • Hosts worried about strangers booking without a check.
  • So the PRD included host rules: e.g. only guests with good reviews or a verified ID can book instantly.

📅 Other calendars

  • Many hosts also list on other websites.
  • The calendar must sync, so a home booked elsewhere can't be instantly booked on Airbnb too.
🎓 TPM lesson from Airbnb: the happy path is easy. A strong PRD spends most of its time on "what if…" — and on the worries of every user group (guests and hosts).

The story: WhatsApp turned on end-to-end encryption for everyone in 2016. Messages are locked on your phone and only unlocked on your friend's phone — not even WhatsApp can read them. The requirements had to balance safety with keeping it simple for 1 billion+ people.

🔒 The main requirement

  • Only the sender and the receiver can read a message.
  • It must be on by default — users shouldn't have to find a setting.

📭 What the servers keep

  • Once a message is delivered, it is deleted from WhatsApp's servers.
  • If the phone is off, the locked message waits on the server for a limited time (about 30 days).

📱 New phone, new lock

  • When a friend gets a new phone, their "lock" changes.
  • WhatsApp shows a small notice in the chat instead of blocking the conversation — safe, but not annoying.

⚖️ Trade-off written down

  • Strong security makes some things harder (e.g. restoring chats on a new phone needs a backup).
  • The PRD has to say clearly which side wins in each case.
🎓 TPM lesson from WhatsApp: non-functional requirements (here, security) can be the whole point of a feature. Write down the trade-offs so the team doesn't argue about them halfway through building.

Saturday class

Questions we will discuss together

Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

1. Why is writing what's "out of scope" just as important as what's in scope?

A good answer: If you don't say what's out, people fill the gap with their own ideas. A designer may spend a week on a feature nobody asked for, or leaders may expect it at launch. "Not in this version" protects the team's time and sets the right expectations.

2. An engineer says: "The PRD says 'fast' — I tested it on my laptop and it's fast." What went wrong?

A good answer: "Fast" isn't measurable, so the engineer chose their own test. Real users have slow phones and weak networks, and arrive 3,000 at a time. The PRD should say: "Loads in under 1 second for 95% of users, with 3,000 orders in the busiest hour."

3. Does a TPM write the whole PRD alone?

A good answer: No. The TPM owns it and writes most of it, but checks it with engineers (Is this possible? How long?), designers (What screens?), QA (Can we test this?) and data (Can we measure this?). A PRD is a shared agreement, not a list of orders.

4. Can a PRD change after engineers start building?

A good answer: Yes — you will learn things while building. But every change should be written in the PRD, with a date and a reason, and shared with the team. Silent changes are how teams end up building different things.
