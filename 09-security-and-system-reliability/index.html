Part 1

"Who are you?" and "What are you allowed to do?"

Think of a hotel. At reception they check your ID and give you a key card — that's authentication: proving who you are.

Your key card only opens your room, not every room — that's authorization: what you're allowed to do. Many real data leaks happen because the second check was forgotten.

🪪 Authentication — "Who are you?"

  • Passwords — never stored as plain text. They're hashed: scrambled one way, so even the company can't read them.
  • MFA (multi-factor) — something you know (password) plus something you have (a code sent to your phone) or are (fingerprint).
  • "Sign in with Google / Apple" — another company checks who you are, so you don't need a new password.
  • After login, the app gets a token — like the key card. It expires, so a stolen one doesn't work forever.

🔑 Authorization — "What can you do?"

  • Roles — a customer, a restaurant owner, a support agent and an admin can each do different things.
  • Ownership — you can see your orders, not other customers'. This must be checked on every request.
  • Least privilege — give each person only the access they need. A support agent doesn't need to export every customer's data.

🎓 A TPM writes these rules into the PRD: "Who can see this? Who can change it?"

Part 2

The most common attacks — in plain words

Security experts at OWASP (a global non-profit) publish lists of the most common ways apps get attacked. You don't need to know how to hack — you need to know what to ask for.

🔢

Changing the number (BOLA / IDOR)

Ada views /orders/1043, changes it to /orders/1044 — and sees a stranger's address and phone number. It's the #1 risk on OWASP's list for APIs.

Fix: on every request, check "does this order belong to the person asking?"

🔁

Trying stolen passwords

Passwords leaked from other websites are tried on your app by bots, millions of times. People reuse passwords, so some work. This is called credential stuffing.

Fix: limit login attempts, and ask for an OTP code on new devices.

💉

Sneaking in commands (injection)

Someone types database commands into a search box. If the app pastes the text straight into a database query, the attacker can read or delete data.

Fix: always treat user input as data, never as commands ("parameterised queries").

🎣

Tricking staff (phishing)

A fake "IT support" email asks a support agent to "confirm your password". Now the attacker can log in as staff — often with more power than any customer.

Fix: MFA for every staff account, and staff get only the access they need.

🗝️

Leaked secrets

A secret API key (Week 3) is left inside the phone app or a public code repository. Anyone who finds it can act as your company.

Fix: secrets stay on the server, and are changed ("rotated") regularly.

🌊

Flooding (DDoS)

Thousands of hacked devices send fake traffic at once, so real customers can't get in. No data is stolen — but the app is down.

Fix: protection at the edge (like a CDN with DDoS protection) and rate limits.

Part 3

Reliability targets: SLI, SLO, SLA and error budgets

No app works 100% of the time. Good teams agree how reliable is reliable enough — and measure it honestly. This practice is often called SRE (Site Reliability Engineering), made popular by Google.

🌡️

SLI — what we measure

Service Level Indicator: the actual number, like a thermometer reading. "% of payments that succeed in under 2 seconds."

🎯

SLO — our target

Service Level Objective: the goal the team agrees on. "99.9% of payments succeed, measured over 30 days."

📜

SLA — our promise

Service Level Agreement: a promise in a contract, often with money back if it's broken. Usually a bit lower than the SLO, to leave a safety margin.

⏳ Try it: what does each target allow?

The "error budget" is the downtime your target allows

      Part 5

      Real examples: Apple and Cloudflare

      One company protects data on your phone. The other protects millions of websites from floods of attack traffic.

      The story: iPhones hold very sensitive things: your face or fingerprint data, cards for Apple Pay, passwords. Apple designs privacy and security in from the start, so even Apple can't see some of it.

      🔐 A separate safe inside the phone

      • iPhones have a Secure Enclave: a separate, locked-off part of the chip.
      • Face ID and fingerprint data are protected there and don't leave the device — even the rest of the phone's software can't read them.

      💳 Apple Pay doesn't share your card number

      • Shops receive a special one-off code instead of your real card number.
      • So a shop that gets hacked can't leak your actual card (the same "token" idea as Week 3).

      🕵🏽 Private Relay: nobody sees everything

      • iCloud Private Relay sends Safari traffic through two separate relays.
      • The first (Apple's) sees who you are but not where you're going; the second (run by partners like Cloudflare) sees where you're going but not who you are.

      🧾 Keep less data

      • The safest data is data you never collect.
      • Doing work on the device, instead of sending everything to servers, means less to lose.
      🎓 TPM lesson from Apple: privacy is a product feature. In every PRD, ask: do we need to collect this at all? And if we do, who can see it?

      The story: Cloudflare sits in front of millions of websites as a CDN (Week 8) and a shield. In February 2023 it reported stopping a flood of over 71 million requests per second — a record at the time.

      🌍 Spread the flood out

      • Cloudflare has data centres in over 300 cities.
      • Attack traffic lands at the nearest one, so the flood is split up and absorbed in many places — never all at your server.

      🤖 Spot bots automatically

      • Systems look for patterns that real people don't make (thousands of identical requests a second).
      • Suspicious traffic is blocked or challenged before it reaches your app.

      🚪 "Zero trust" for staff

      • Old idea: everyone inside the office network is trusted.
      • Zero trust: every person and device is checked on every request — wherever they are.

      🔑 Hardware security keys

      • Cloudflare has written about staff using physical security keys for MFA.
      • In 2022 a phishing campaign targeted its staff — and the keys stopped it, because a stolen password alone wasn't enough.
      🎓 TPM lesson from Cloudflare: assume attacks will happen. Design so that one stolen password, or one flood of traffic, isn't enough to cause harm.

      Saturday class

      Questions we will discuss together

      Think about your answer first, then tap to see one good answer.

      1. Security work slows down feature launches. How do you argue for it?

      A good answer: Talk about risk in business terms: "If someone can read other people's orders, we may expose 50,000 customers, face regulators, and lose trust that took years to build." Then show it's targeted: "These 3 fixes cover our most likely, most harmful threats, for 2 sprints of work."

      2. Why not aim for 100% reliability?

      A good answer: Because each extra "nine" gets much more expensive, and customers can't tell the difference past a point — their own phone network fails more often. A realistic SLO leaves an error budget, so the team can keep shipping features. If the budget runs out, reliability work comes first.

      3. A support agent asks for access to export all customer data "just in case". What do you say?

      A good answer: Politely no — least privilege. Ask what problem they're solving and give the smallest access that solves it (for example, searching one customer at a time). Every extra permission is something an attacker can use if that account is phished.

      4. ChopNow finds out customer data was exposed. What should happen in the first 72 hours?

      A good answer: Close the hole first, then find out exactly what was exposed and for how many people. Under Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023, the Nigeria Data Protection Commission must be told within 72 hours, and affected people told quickly if they're at high risk. Be honest and clear with customers — hiding it makes everything worse.
